# Novel Writing Tips

- Show through specific action, sensory detail and subtext; avoid stating emotions or themes outright.
- Open scenes late and leave early. Every scene needs a goal, a conflict and a change.
- Keep point of view and tense consistent within a scene; never leak knowledge the POV character lacks.
- Make dialogue sound distinct per character. Cut greetings, filler and on-the-nose exposition.
- Vary sentence length and openers. Avoid stacked adverbs, filter words (felt, saw, noticed) and cliché.
- Ground each scene early: where, when, who is present.
- Plant and pay off: track setups, clues and promises already made in the story.
- End chapters on a turn, question or shifted stakes, not a summary.


## Acts and Chapters

**Acts are a planning tool for the shape of the whole book. Chapters are the units that carry out each act's job.**

Acts exist so the story keeps escalating and changing direction at the right moments, rather than drifting or repeating itself. The usual three-act shape:

- **Act 1: Setup.** Establish the protagonist, their world, what they want and what is wrong. An inciting incident disrupts normal life, and the act ends when the protagonist commits to a course of action and cannot easily go back.
- **Act 2: Confrontation.** The protagonist pursues the goal against escalating obstacles. Complications deepen, a midpoint shift changes what the reader or character believes is happening, and things reach a low point where the goal seems lost or costs more than expected.
- **Act 3: Resolution.** The protagonist makes a final choice, the central conflict reaches its climax, and the consequences play out. The ending answers the story's central question and shows how things are different from the start.

**How chapters serve acts**

- Every chapter should advance the job of its act: setting up in Act 1, complicating and escalating in Act 2, resolving in Act 3.
- Act breaks should land on a decisive turn, not just a change of scene. The last chapter of an act should shift the stakes or the direction of the story.
- Chapters within an act should vary in intensity and shape, but each one should leave something different from where it began.
- Acts do not need equal length. Act 2 is usually the longest, and Acts 1 and 3 are often shorter and tighter.
- Use acts to check pacing: if Act 2 feels flat, find where escalation stalled; if Act 3 feels rushed, check whether Act 2 earned the climax.


## Openings

**Give the reader one thing they understand, one thing they don't understand, and one reason to care.**

A strong opening does not need to explain everything. It should create enough clarity for the reader to orient themselves, while withholding something that makes them want to continue.

### The three-part opening

**1. One thing the reader understands**  
Give them something concrete: a person, place, action, situation or immediate fact.

> Alison was waiting outside the hotel.

The reader knows who, where and what is happening.

**2. One thing the reader doesn't understand**  
Introduce a question, contradiction, secret or unexplained detail.

> Alison was waiting outside the hotel, although she had checked out two hours earlier.

Now the reader has a question: **Why did she come back?**

**3. One reason to care**  
Give the situation emotional or narrative significance.

> Alison was waiting outside the hotel, although she had checked out two hours earlier. She was waiting for the man who had told her, twenty years ago, that he was dead.

Now there is a reason to keep reading. The reader wants to know who the man is, why he said he was dead, and what Alison intends to do.

### Ten useful opening approaches

- **Start with a startling statement:** make the reader immediately ask how or why.
- **Start in the middle of an action:** arrive late, when something is already happening.
- **Start with a striking image:** give the reader something visually or emotionally memorable.
- **Start with dialogue:** establish character and tension through a distinctive voice.
- **Start with a secret:** let the reader know something important is being concealed.
- **Start with a contradiction:** present something that doesn't quite make sense.
- **Start quietly:** establish ordinary life, then introduce a disturbance.
- **Start with a memory:** suggest that something in the past is about to become important.
- **Start inside a character's thought:** immediately establish personality or emotional conflict.
- **Start at the moment everything changes:** show the point at which life moves from before to after.

The opening should also **ground the scene early**: where, when and who is present. The reader should not be confused simply because the writer has withheld basic orientation.

A useful test is:

> **Clarity + Question + Emotional stake.**

If the reader understands everything, there may be no reason to continue. If the reader understands nothing, there may be no reason to care.

## Pacing & Beats

A useful way to control pacing is **PPP: Promise, Progress, Payoff.**

### Promise

Give the reader something they expect to develop.

This might be:
- a question
- a threat
- a relationship
- a secret
- a goal
- a clue
- a problem
- an emotional possibility

The promise does not have to be explicit. Often the strongest promises are created through implication.

> Gerald looked at the unopened letter and put it back in the drawer.

The reader immediately expects the letter to matter.

### Progress

Move the promise forward.

Something should change, deepen, complicate or become more urgent. Progress can come through:
- new information
- an obstacle
- a decision
- a reversal
- a discovery
- a change in a relationship
- increasing consequences

Progress does not mean constant action. A quiet conversation can have enormous narrative progress if what the characters understand about each other changes.

### Payoff

Deliver something that answers, fulfils or transforms the original promise.

The payoff can be:
- an answer
- a revelation
- a confrontation
- a consequence
- a reversal
- an emotional realisation

Then create the **next promise**.

This produces a chain:

> **Promise → Progress → Payoff → New Promise**

That chain is one of the basic engines of narrative momentum.

A chapter can contain several small PPP cycles inside a larger one. The important thing is that the reader should feel that the story is **going somewhere**.

The existing principle remains important: plant things and pay them off, and track the promises and clues already made in the story.

## Characters

Readers invest in characters because they **want something, fear something, care about something, or have something at stake**.

A character does not need to be likeable. They need to be **interesting, emotionally legible and consequential**.

### Why readers invest

Readers become invested when they can:

- **Understand the character's desire:** What do they want right now?
- **Understand the stakes:** What happens if they don't get it?
- **See vulnerability:** What can hurt them emotionally?
- **Recognise contradiction:** What do they say they want versus what they actually want?
- **See agency:** What choices do they make rather than simply having things happen to them?
- **Want to know what happens to them:** Their future has become personally important to the reader.

### How to create investment

**Give the character a want early.**

It can be small:

> Alison wanted him to call before midnight.

But it should matter to her.

**Give them something to lose.**

The reader needs to understand what failure costs.

**Show rather than label emotion.**

Use specific action, sensory detail and subtext rather than simply telling the reader what the character feels.

Instead of:

> Alison was nervous.

Use behaviour:

> Alison read the message twice, deleted it, then typed the same three words again.

**Give characters contradictions.**

A character who is entirely predictable quickly becomes flat.

The charming man who is terrified of intimacy is more interesting than simply a charming man. The ruthless businessman who cannot bear to disappoint his daughter contains a contradiction the reader can explore.

**Let characters make choices.**

Readers become invested when characters have agency. Their decisions should create consequences, not merely react to events.

**Give every important character a distinct voice.**

Dialogue should sound different from character to character, and greetings, filler and obvious exposition should be removed.

## The End

A chapter ending should **change the reader's understanding or expectations**.

Do not simply stop when the scene has run out of material. End when something has shifted.

Strong chapter endings can:

- reveal something
- raise a new question
- change the stakes
- reverse what the reader thought was happening
- force a character to make a decision
- introduce a threat
- reveal a consequence
- create an emotional turn
- promise the next piece of the story

The ideal ending often makes the reader think:

> **I need to know what happens next.**

It does not necessarily have to be a cliffhanger. A quiet revelation can be just as powerful as an explosion.

For example:

> Gerald closed the door behind him.  
>   
> Then Alison saw the blood on his sleeve.

The scene has ended, but the story has just opened another question.

A chapter should generally **end on a turn, question or shifted stake rather than a summary**. 

### The chapter's overall rhythm

A useful chapter shape is:

> **Opening → Promise → Progress → Complication → Payoff/Turn → New Promise**

Not every chapter needs every stage in equal measure. Some chapters may be quiet, reflective or primarily character-driven. But something should change between the first and last page.

The simplest test is:

> **At the end of the chapter, what is different from the beginning?**
